What Is My Hair Type? A 5-Question Quiz to Find Out

Updated November 9, 2026 · 10 min read

Your hair type comes down to three separate traits: the shape your hair dries in (straight, wavy, curly or coily), how thick each strand is, and how much hair you have overall. This quiz walks you through each one with a simple check you can do at home, then turns your answers into styling and haircut direction. It takes about ten minutes and needs a mirror, daylight and a piece of sewing thread.

How this "what is my hair type" quiz works

Hair type, texture and density: what each word means

People use these words loosely, so here is how this guide uses them:

  • Pattern (often called hair type): the shape your hair forms as it dries, from straight to tightly coiled.
  • Strand thickness (often called texture): whether individual strands are fine, medium or coarse.
  • Density: how many strands grow from your scalp, which is low, medium or high.

Two people can both have wavy hair and need completely different cuts because one has fine, low-density strands and the other has coarse, high-density hair. That is why this quiz treats them as separate questions.

Why the labels are a starting point, not a verdict

The 1A to 4C system is an informal shorthand that stylists and hair communities use. It is not a scientific classification, and most people have more than one pattern on their head. Use your result to describe your hair and narrow down styles, not as a box you have to fit into.

Before you start: set up so your answers are accurate

Start with clean, product-free, air-dried hair

Wash your hair with your usual shampoo, skip stylers, and let it air-dry completely. Heat tools, gels, oils and humidity all change how hair looks, so a quiz taken on blow-dried or product-coated hair will mislead you. If you can, wait until you have not used heat for a day or two.

Check in natural light and look at several sections

Stand near a window and look at your front hairline, crown, sides and nape separately. Your hair often behaves differently in each area, and noting that now makes Question 2 easier.

Question 1: What shape does your hair dry in?

Look at the mid-lengths and ends of your dry hair, not the roots.

Straight: falls flat from root to tip

Your hair hangs without bending, apart from a slight flick at the ends. Straight hair shows sharp, blunt lines clearly, but it can struggle to hold curls without help.

Wavy: forms a loose S-shape

Your hair bends in gentle S-shapes, often starting a little below the roots. It sits between flat and curly, and it can look straighter when it is long and heavier.

Curly: forms defined spirals or ringlets

Your hair springs into spirals or ringlets, from loose loops to tight corkscrews. Curls often look shorter than they really are when dry, because the strand pulls up as it coils.

Coily: forms tight coils or zig-zag patterns

Your hair forms very tight coils, small springs or sharp zig-zag bends, and it may look much shorter than its stretched length. Many people with coily hair have a mix of coil sizes across the head.

Question 2: How tight is the curl or wave?

Wide, medium or tight pattern, and what 1A to 4C labels try to capture

The numbers match your Question 1 answer (1 is straight, 2 wavy, 3 curly, 4 coily). The letters describe how wide or tight the pattern is, with A being the loosest and C the tightest. Here is a rough way to place yourself:

  • Wide: loose waves or large loops, roughly A.
  • Medium: clear S-shapes or curls about the width of a marker barrel, roughly B.
  • Tight: small curls or coils, roughly C.

The marker comparison is only an approximate guide, and the boundaries between A, B and C are informal, so a near match is good enough.

Why you may have different patterns in different sections

It is common to have looser waves at the front and tighter curls at the nape, or straighter lengths with a curlier underlayer. Pick the pattern that covers most of your head and note any exceptions. You do not need a perfect letter and number.

Question 3: Are your strands fine, medium or coarse?

The single-strand check against a piece of sewing thread

Take one clean, dry strand and hold it next to a piece of ordinary sewing thread. A shed hair from your brush or clothing works well, so there is no need to pull one out.

  • Thinner than the thread: fine.
  • About the same: medium.
  • Thicker than the thread: coarse.

You can also roll the strand between your fingers. Fine strands are barely noticeable, while coarse ones feel distinct. Fine hair tends to go limp under heavy products, while coarse hair usually holds weight and shape well but can take longer to style.

Why strand thickness is not the same as how much hair you have

You can have fine strands and a lot of them, or coarse strands and only a few. Thickness describes the individual strand, and the next question covers how many strands there are.

Question 4: How dense is your hair?

The ponytail and scalp-visibility checks

Try two quick checks:

  1. Scalp visibility: part your dry hair in the middle. If your scalp shows clearly right away, density is likely lower. If you have to part carefully to see it, density is likely higher.
  2. Ponytail feel: gather your hair into a low ponytail. A slim ponytail that a hair tie wraps around several times suggests lower density. One that fills your hand and a tie wraps only once or twice suggests higher density.

Low, medium and high density in plain language

Low density means a slim ponytail and easy scalp visibility. Medium sits in between. High density means a full ponytail and a scalp that is hard to see. This is a style question only: it affects which cuts look full and how much volume a style gives you.

Question 5: How does your hair behave in humidity and after shampooing?

Frizz, shrinkage and how long a style holds

Over a day or two, and on a damp day if you can, notice what your hair does. Then pick the closest match:

  • Swells and frizzes in humidity (A): smoothing or anti-frizz styling usually helps.
  • Shrinks noticeably as it dries (B): plan cut length with shrinkage in mind.
  • Looks good after shampooing but flat by day two (C): lightweight products and some root lift help.
  • Looks much the same all day (D): you have more freedom with styles.

After shampooing, compare wash day with day two or three. Check whether your pattern shows clearly when freshly washed and air-dried, how much volume you have, and how long a style lasts. Hair that swells in humidity and hair that falls flat by lunchtime need different styling approaches, even if the pattern is the same.

What coloring, heat and chemical treatments can change in how your hair looks and behaves

Coloring, bleaching, smoothing treatments and regular heat styling can all change how your hair looks and behaves, including how well a curl or wave forms. If your answers do not match what you remember from years ago, treatments may be part of the reason. A hairdresser can tell you what they see in person.

Haircut and styling ideas for straight, wavy, curly and coily hair

  • Straight: a sleek blunt bob, a long one-length cut or curtain bangs. Texture spray adds grip.
  • Wavy: a shoulder-length cut with soft layers, or a shag if your hair is medium to thick.
  • Curly: a layered cut that keeps curls balanced rather than triangle-shaped. Ask your hairdresser how they cut curls, since some cut them dry.
  • Coily: a tapered cut, a rounded shape or a longer style that is stretched for versatility.

How to describe your hair to a hairdresser in one sentence

Combine your answers: "I have medium-width curls, fine strands and low density, and I want a cut that adds volume without losing length." Bring two or three reference photos of cuts you like on a similar pattern.

Common mix-ups and edge cases

Wavy or curly? How to decide when you are in between

If a section holds a clear S-shape but does not form a closed loop, call it wavy. If it spirals into loops once it is product-free and air-dried, call it curly. If your hair does both, you can describe it as wavy-curly. Hairdressers usually care more about how it behaves than which label you choose.

Why your hair may look different from season to season or after a cut or color

Humidity, a recent haircut, the weight of your length and color treatments can all change how your pattern appears. Retake the quiz if your hair seems to have changed noticeably, and always under the same conditions: clean, product-free and air-dried.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have more than one hair type?

Yes, and it is very common. You might have waves at the front, curls at the crown and looser strands at the nape. Describe the pattern that covers most of your head and mention the exceptions to your hairdresser.

Does hair type change over time?

Your hair can look and behave differently over the years. Haircuts, coloring, heat styling, humidity and changes in length can all affect how a wave or curl shows up. If your hair seems different, retake the quiz on clean, air-dried hair, and see a doctor if you have concerns about changes in your hair or scalp.

Is hair type the same as hair texture?

Not quite, although people often use the words interchangeably. Hair type usually refers to the pattern, from straight to coily, while texture often means strand thickness, such as fine, medium or coarse. When you talk to a stylist, it is clearest to say pattern, strand thickness and density separately.

Do I need to know my 1A to 4C number?

No. The system is informal shorthand and many people find that no single label fits their whole head. A short description, such as medium curls, fine strands and low density, is usually more helpful than a number.

How often should I retake the quiz?

There is no need to retake it regularly. Try it again if your hair has changed after a cut, color or treatment, or if you want to double-check your answers. Always test under the same conditions: clean, product-free and air-dried.
