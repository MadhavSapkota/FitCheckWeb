What Color Undertones Do I Have? 5 Simple Tests to Find Out

By FitCheck Team · Published November 18, 2026 · Updated November 18, 2026 · 9 min read

Your undertone is the subtle, steady hue beneath your skin's surface color. It is usually described as cool, warm, neutral or olive, though labels vary a little between guides. You can work out your likely undertone at home with a few quick checks in natural light, then count your results instead of trusting any single test. Treat the answer as a styling starting point, not a rule.

Undertone in 30 seconds: what it is and why it matters

Undertone is the hue under the surface of your skin. It is why one lipstick looks fresh on you and another looks slightly off, or why one white shirt looks crisp while another looks harsh near your face. Knowing it narrows down the colors to try first, which saves time when you shop for clothes, jewelry and makeup.

Undertone vs. surface skin tone (and why a tan doesn't change it)

Skin tone, often called depth, is how light or deep your skin looks overall. Undertone is the hue underneath. Two people can share the same depth and have different undertones. A tan or a flush of redness changes the surface, but the underlying hue is generally considered stable. That is also why a tan can make you misread your result. To sort out depth separately, see our guide What Is My Skin Tone?

The four labels you'll see: cool, warm, neutral, olive

  • Cool: a pink, rosy or bluish cast underneath.
  • Warm: a yellow, peachy or golden cast underneath.
  • Neutral: a fairly even balance of cool and warm, with neither clearly winning.
  • Olive: a muted, slightly greenish or grayish-gold cast, usually with some warmth mixed in. Some guides treat olive as its own category, while others file it under neutral or warm, so don't worry if you see it labeled differently elsewhere.

Set up first so you read your skin fairly

Most wrong answers come from poor conditions, not poor judgment. Spend two minutes on setup before any test.

Use natural daylight, near a window, with no filters

Stand facing a window in the daytime. Avoid warm yellow bulbs, cool LED strips and direct harsh sun, which all shift how skin looks. If you check with a phone camera, turn off beauty filters and automatic smoothing.

Go bare-faced and take off colored clothing and bold jewelry

Remove foundation, tinted moisturizer and blush if you can. Wear a plain white or neutral top, and move away from brightly painted walls, which can bounce color onto your face.

Why redness, a fresh tan or colored lighting can mislead you

Temporary flushing can make a warm or neutral person look cool and pink. A fresh tan can make a cool person look golden. If either applies, check an area that sees less sun, such as the inner arm, and consider re-checking in a few weeks. This is cosmetic reading only. If you have any concern about your skin itself, ask a doctor or dermatologist.

5 simple tests to find your undertone

Do all five and jot down a result for each: cool, warm, neutral or unsure. No single test is conclusive.

Test 1: The vein test (and its limits)

Look at the inside of your wrist in daylight. Veins that look bluish or purple lean cool. Veins that look greenish lean warm. If you see both, note "neutral." The limits: vein color is affected by skin depth, lighting and how close the vein sits to the surface, so many people honestly can't tell. If you can't, write "unsure," and treat this as a weak vote either way.

Test 2: The gold vs. silver jewelry test

Hold a gold piece and a silver piece against your bare skin near your face or jawline, one at a time, or one on each side if you have a friend to help. Which makes your skin look brighter and more even? Silver often flatters cool undertones, gold often flatters warm ones, and neutral skin tends to look fine in both. Rose gold sits in between and often suits cool and neutral skin.

Test 3: The white vs. off-white fabric test

Hold a stark, bright white fabric against your face, then a cream or ivory one. If bright white looks crisp and cream looks slightly dull or yellow on you, you may lean cool. If cream looks rich and bright white feels harsh, you may lean warm. If both work, note neutral.

Test 4: How your skin looks next to different colors

Hold solid pieces under your chin, one at a time. Compare pairs such as:

  • Icy pink vs. peach
  • Royal blue vs. camel
  • Emerald vs. olive green
  • Burgundy vs. rust

If the first of each pair makes your face look clearer and more even, vote cool. If the second does, vote warm. If neither set clearly wins, vote neutral or unsure.

Test 5: Which foundation or concealer shades seem to vanish into your skin

If you own a few shades, swatch them along your jawline in daylight. The one that disappears is the closest match. Pink or rosy bases that blend suggest cool, yellow or golden bases suggest warm, and a mix of both suggests neutral. Bases with a slightly muted, greenish-gold look that blend may point toward olive. Product undertone labels vary by brand, so compare this result with your other tests.

Add up your results: cool, warm, neutral or olive

A simple tally: how to read your answers

  1. Write down your vote from each of the five tests: cool, warm, neutral or unsure.
  2. Set the "unsure" votes aside. They are not neutral votes, just tests that didn't tell you anything.
  3. If cool or warm has three or more votes, that is your likely undertone.
  4. If neutral has three or more votes, or cool and warm are tied, your likely undertone is neutral.
  5. If three or more tests came out "unsure," repeat the setup in better light and lean on Tests 3 and 4, which show how colors behave near your face.

What a neutral undertone looks like in practice

Neutral means your skin carries both cool and warm hues without one clearly leading. In practice, you might look good in both gold and silver, find that both bright white and cream work, and see blue-green veins. You often have a wider range to play with, and depth and contrast will steer your choices more than undertone does.

What olive undertone means and how it differs from neutral

Olive is a distinct case, not just a synonym for warm. It usually looks muted, with a slight green or gray-gold cast. Olive skin can look flat next to very pink or very orange shades, and it often does well with earthy, saturated colors such as emerald, deep teal, rust and warm burgundy. Neutral is a balanced mix, while olive has that specific greenish, muted quality. If your tests are mixed and you notice that muted cast, olive may be the better label for you.

When your tests disagree: which ones to trust less

Trust the vein test least, because lighting and vein depth affect it. Give more weight to the color-against-face comparison (Test 4) and the fabric test, because they show how colors actually look near your face, which is what matters for styling. If you are still torn, go with "neutral" for now and rely on what looks best in the mirror.

Jewelry and metal tones

Cool: silver, platinum, white gold. Warm: yellow gold, brass, copper. Neutral: gold, silver or rose gold, or mixed metals. Olive: antique or yellow gold often looks rich, but silver can work if the piece is bold and clean.

Makeup: foundation, blush and lipstick starting points

  • Cool: foundation with a pink or neutral-pink base, rose or berry blush, blue-red or raspberry lipstick.
  • Warm: golden or peachy foundation, peach or apricot blush, coral, brick or warm red lipstick.
  • Neutral: balanced foundation, soft rose or peach blush, mauve or rosy-nude lipstick.
  • Olive: foundation with a slight golden-olive balance, warm terracotta or soft coral blush, brick, deep berry or warm nude lipstick.

Colors outside your "palette": how to wear them with confidence

You can wear any color you love. A few tricks help:

  • Placement: put a tricky color on the bottom half, such as trousers, a skirt or shoes, and keep a flattering color near your face.
  • A flattering layer near the face: a neutral tee, scarf or necklace between the color and your skin softens the effect.
  • Adjust the shade: if bright orange is harsh on you, try a deeper rust. If icy pink looks washed out, try a dusty rose.
  • Use makeup as a bridge: a lip or blush color that echoes the garment can tie it together.

Undertone is a starting point, not a rulebook

Why contrast level, depth and personal taste matter too

Undertone is one factor among several. Contrast (how different your hair, skin and eyes are from one another) affects whether you suit bold color blocking or softer, tonal looks. Depth affects how rich or pale a color can be before it overwhelms you. And preference counts: if a color makes you feel great, it is working.

Skin depth is a separate question

Depth and undertone are different questions, and both matter when choosing foundation or deciding how light or deep a color should be. Our skin tone guide covers depth in full, so you can combine it with what you learned here.

Frequently asked questions

Can my undertone change over time?

Your undertone is generally considered stable, but how your skin looks can shift. Tanning, redness and changes in hair color can make it seem like your undertone has changed, so re-test in natural light with a bare face before deciding. If you notice skin changes that worry you, speak to a doctor or dermatologist.

Can I have a different undertone on my face and body?

It can look that way, since the face often has more redness and sun exposure than the rest of the body. For choosing colors near your face, go with what you see on your face and neck in daylight. For body makeup or self-tanner, check a less sun-exposed area such as the inner arm.

Is neutral the same as olive?

Not exactly, though labels vary and some guides group olive under neutral or warm. Neutral means your cool and warm hues are fairly balanced. Olive usually has a muted, slightly greenish or gray-gold cast with some warmth. They can be hard to tell apart, so compare how emerald, rust and dusty rose look next to your face.

Do I have to avoid colors that aren't in my undertone's palette?

No. Undertone is a starting point, and personal taste matters just as much. If you love a color that isn't on your list, wear it away from your face, add a flattering layer near your neckline, or try a slightly deeper or softer version of the shade.

Which test is the most reliable?

No single test is reliable on its own. The color-against-face comparison and the fabric test are usually more helpful than the vein test, because they show how colors actually look near your face. The best approach is to run several tests and go with the result that appears most often.
