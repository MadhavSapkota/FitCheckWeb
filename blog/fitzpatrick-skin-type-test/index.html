Fitzpatrick Skin Type Test: An Informal Self-Quiz and What Your Result Means

By FitCheck Team · Published November 25, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026 · 9 min read

A Fitzpatrick skin type test sorts people into one of six types, I to VI, based on natural skin coloring and how skin tends to react to sun. The version below is a short, informal self-quiz. It is not the clinical questionnaire, so treat the result as a rough category for curiosity and style, not a medical assessment.

This guide gives you a quick quiz, explains what each type describes, and shows where the scale helps and where it falls short. It also covers how to move from the number to cosmetic decisions such as makeup and color choices, where undertone and depth matter far more.

What is the Fitzpatrick skin type scale?

The Fitzpatrick scale is a six-point system that groups skin by natural coloring and typical reaction to sun, from Type I (very fair, burns easily) to Type VI (deeply pigmented, rarely if ever burns). It describes a broad pattern, not your exact shade or undertone.

The six types (I to VI) in plain language

  • Type I: very fair skin that burns easily and does not tan.
  • Type II: fair skin that burns easily and tans only slightly.
  • Type III: light to medium skin that sometimes burns and tans gradually.
  • Type IV: olive or light brown skin that burns minimally and tans easily.
  • Type V: medium to deep brown skin that rarely burns and tans darkly.
  • Type VI: deep brown to very deep skin that rarely, if ever, burns noticeably.

If you searched for a Fitzpatrick skin types chart or "skin type 1 to 6", that list is the short version. Note that this is a different use of "skin type" than oily, dry or combination.

What the scale measures: how skin tends to react to sun

At its core, the scale is about reaction: does your skin tend to burn, tan, or both? That is why the quiz below leans on memories of past sun exposure rather than on how your skin looks today.

What the scale does not measure: undertone, shade, or skin health

The scale does not tell you whether your undertone is warm, cool or neutral. It does not give you a foundation shade, and it says nothing about the condition of your skin. Two people with the same type can look quite different side by side.

Sun-safety note: every skin type can burn and be damaged by the sun. Do not use your result, or phrases like "rarely burns", to decide how much sun protection you need. A dermatologist or doctor can advise you on that.

How do I set up to answer honestly?

Think about your skin as it naturally is, without self-tanner or recent tanning, and answer based on what usually happens, not one unusual day.

Think about untreated skin and typical reactions

For the color question, look at a spot that rarely sees the sun, such as the inner upper arm, in daylight near a window. Indoor bulbs can make skin look more yellow, pink or gray than it is. If you can't remember how your skin reacted to sun, pick the closest answer and treat your result as less certain.

Why memory-based answers are only approximate

The quiz relies on recalling past reactions, and memory is imperfect. Sunscreen use, shade, season and time of day all change what happened, so keep your expectations modest.

Fitzpatrick skin type test: a 4-question informal quiz

This is a simplified, non-standard quiz written for self-reflection. The clinical questionnaire is different: it uses weighted points across several traits. Eye color and hair color are left out here on purpose, because they can pull people with deep skin and light eyes, or fair skin and dark hair, toward the wrong type.

For each question, pick the closest answer and note the type in brackets.

Question 1: natural skin color

What is your natural skin color on a rarely exposed area?

  • Very pale or ivory (I)
  • Fair (II)
  • Light to medium beige (III)
  • Olive or light brown (IV)
  • Medium to deep brown (V)
  • Deep to very deep brown (VI)

Question 2: burning

After a first extended stretch of unprotected sun, how does your skin usually react?

  • Burns quickly, often painfully, and peels (I)
  • Burns easily and usually peels (II)
  • Burns moderately (III)
  • Burns minimally (IV)
  • Rarely burns (V)
  • Rarely, if ever, burns noticeably (VI)

Question 3: tanning

How does your skin tend to tan, and how quickly?

  • Does not tan; may freckle or turn red (I)
  • Tans lightly or barely (II)
  • Tans gradually to a light brown (III)
  • Tans easily to a moderate brown (IV)
  • Tans quickly and darkly (V)
  • Deepens naturally, and the change is hard to see because skin is already very deep (VI)

Question 4: how your skin looks after repeated sun

After weeks of regular sun exposure over a summer, what do you notice?

  • Mostly redness and freckles, little color change (I)
  • A slight, short-lived tan (II)
  • A light-to-medium tan that builds slowly (III)
  • A clear tan that builds easily and lasts (IV)
  • A deep tan that builds quickly and lasts a long time (V)
  • Little visible change, because skin is already very deep (VI)

How to tally your answers, and what to do with a tie

Write down the four types you picked. If most answers point to the same type, that is your rough type. If your answers split between neighbors, such as III and IV, give more weight to questions 2 to 4, since the scale is built around sun reaction. If you are still undecided, describe yourself as "between III and IV". That is a perfectly reasonable answer for style purposes. This tally is informal and has no scientific validation behind it.

What do my results mean?

Your result places you in a broad group of people whose skin tends to react to sun in a similar way. For style purposes, it is background context, not a final label.

Types I and II

These types usually have fair skin that burns easily. Shades and undertones still vary a lot within this range, so use depth and undertone, not the number, when choosing blush intensity or outfit colors.

Types III and IV

These are the middle of the scale: light-to-medium or olive and light brown skin that tans more readily. This group covers a wide range of tones and undertones, which is why the number alone tells you little about foundation or palette.

Types V and VI

These types describe medium-deep to very deep skin. The scale is much less detailed here, so the range of shades and undertones inside these two numbers is large. A lower chance of visible burning does not mean skin is immune to sun damage.

Why your result is a rough category, not a precise label

Skin color changes with season and sun exposure, and the scale is coarse. This quiz is not medical advice. If you have concerns about your skin, moles, skin changes or how your skin reacts to sun, please see a dermatologist or doctor.

Is Fitzpatrick type the same as skin tone or undertone?

No. Fitzpatrick type describes sun reaction, depth describes how light or deep your skin is, and undertone describes the subtle warm, cool or neutral cast beneath the surface.

Why two people with the same type can need different foundation or palettes

Imagine two people who both land on Type III. One has a golden, peachy undertone and may look best in warm olive green or camel. The other has a rosy undertone and may prefer cool berry or navy. The number is identical, but their color choices differ because undertone does most of the work.

Where the scale falls short, especially for deeper skin tones

The scale lumps a very wide range of deeper skin tones into a few numbers, so it is not a good guide to shade or undertone for those groups. It also depends on memory and self-report. For picking colors, depth and undertone give you far more to work with.

How can I use this for makeup and color choices?

Start with undertone and depth, and treat your Fitzpatrick number as loose context only. It should not pick your shade.

Start from undertone and depth

A practical rule of thumb: match foundation to your depth first, then to your undertone. For clothing, warm undertones often suit olive, rust, cream and mustard, while cool undertones often suit navy, emerald, plum and crisp white. Neutral undertones can usually wear both. These are tendencies, not laws.

Test foundation and clothing colors in natural light

Swatch foundation along the jawline and check it in daylight after a few minutes, since some formulas shift as they settle. For clothes, hold a garment under your chin near a window. If your face looks bright and your skin looks even, it is likely working. If you look tired or washed out, try a different shade.

Treat any palette advice as a starting point

Personal taste, hair color, eye color and the occasion all matter. Use any palette advice, including from this article, as a starting point you adjust to what you enjoy wearing.

Frequently asked questions

Can my Fitzpatrick skin type change?

The scale is meant to describe a general tendency, so it is not supposed to shift from week to week. Your visible skin color can change with seasons and sun exposure, which may make your answers feel different at different times. If your answers fall between two types, it is fine to note both.

Does Fitzpatrick type tell me my undertone?

No. Fitzpatrick type is about how skin tends to react to sun, while undertone is the warm, cool or neutral cast beneath the surface. People with the same type can have different undertones, so use separate undertone checks for makeup and color choices.

Is the Fitzpatrick scale the same as the skin tone charts used for makeup?

No. Makeup shade ranges are built around depth and undertone and usually have many more steps than six. The Fitzpatrick scale is a coarse grouping, so it is a weak guide for picking a foundation shade.
