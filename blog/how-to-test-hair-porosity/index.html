How to Test Hair Porosity at Home (and What to Do With the Result)

By FitCheck Team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 24, 2026 · 8 min read

To test hair porosity at home, start with clean, product-free hair and try two or three simple checks: the float test, the slip-and-slide strand test and the spray bottle test. Compare the results to see whether your hair tends toward low, medium or high porosity. These tests are rough guides, not lab measurements, so treat the outcome as a starting point for choosing products and styling habits.

What is hair porosity, and what isn't it?

Porosity describes how readily your hair takes in and holds onto moisture. Each strand is covered in overlapping scales called the cuticle. When the cuticle lies flat and tight, water and products tend to enter slowly. When it is raised or uneven, they tend to enter quickly and may leave just as fast.

Porosity vs. hair type, strand width and density

Porosity is not the same as hair type. Your type (straight, wavy, curly or coily) describes the shape of the strand and the curl pattern. Strand width is how thick each hair is, and density is how many hairs you have. Two people with identical curl patterns can have very different porosity. If you are not sure of your type yet, take the hair type quiz first, then come back here.

Why people want to know it

The practical reason is product choice and routine: how heavy a cream to use, how to apply it, and how much heat feels right. It is a styling characteristic, not a measure of whether your hair or scalp is healthy.

How do I prep to get a fair porosity result?

Many unreliable results come from skipping preparation. Product residue can change how water behaves on a strand, and oils can affect whether it floats, which can skew almost every test.

Start with clean hair free of oils, conditioner and styling products

Wash with a clarifying or regular shampoo, rinse well, and let your hair dry completely without applying conditioner, oil, leave-in or gel. Wait until the next day if you can, so you are testing your hair and not your products.

Collect strands from different areas

Use a few strands rather than just one, and pull them from different areas if possible. Hair near your crown, your nape and the ends of your lengths can behave differently, especially if parts of your hair are colored or heat-styled. Strands that have shed naturally, for example from your brush, are the easiest to collect, but they may not behave exactly like hair that is still on your head, and hair snipped from your head may give a slightly different result again. Treat any sample as a rough guide.

Test more than once and use more than one method

One test is a snapshot. Running two or three methods, and repeating one of them on another day, gives you a fuller picture than any single result.

Test 1: The float test (treat it with caution)

Step-by-step method

  1. Fill a clear glass with room-temperature water.
  2. Drop in a few clean, dry strands (three or four is better than one), ideally from different areas of your head.
  3. Watch for a few minutes without stirring or touching the glass.
  4. Note whether most of the strands float, hover in the middle or sink to the bottom. If they behave differently from each other, note that too.

How people usually read the result

Strands that stay at the top are often read as lower porosity. Strands that sink quickly are often read as higher porosity. Strands that hover in the middle are often read as medium.

Why it can mislead

The float test is popular but far from definitive. Leftover product, natural oils, the weight of a single strand, the surface tension of the water and how long you wait can all change the outcome. Shed hair may also differ from hair cut from your head. A strand that looks like it floats may simply be coated in residue. Use this test as one clue among several.

Test 2: The slip-and-slide strand test

Step-by-step method

  1. Take a clean, dry strand and pinch it between the thumb and forefinger of one hand, near the tip end, to hold it steady.
  2. With the thumb and forefinger of your other hand, pinch the strand a little further along and slide your fingers up the strand toward the root end (the end that was closest to your scalp).
  3. Notice whether your fingers glide smoothly or catch on bumps and roughness.

What the feel generally suggests

A smooth glide is often associated with a tighter, flatter cuticle, which tends to go with lower porosity. A bumpy or rough feel is often associated with a more raised cuticle, which tends to go with higher porosity.

Limits

Roughness can also come from product buildup, heat styling, chemical processing or simply the natural texture of your hair. Because the result is based on feel, it is subjective, so compare it with another test before drawing conclusions.

Test 3: The spray bottle or water absorption test

Step-by-step method

  1. Start with clean, fully dry hair and no products.
  2. Section off a small piece of hair and lightly spritz it with plain water.
  3. Watch what the water does over the next minute or two.

What the result tends to suggest

If water beads up on the surface and sits there before soaking in, your hair may lean toward lower porosity. If the hair soaks up water almost instantly and feels wet through quickly, it may lean toward higher porosity. Gradual, even absorption sits in the middle.

Why it works best on freshly clean hair

Any coating on the hair can make water bead up, so this test is only meaningful on freshly cleansed strands. Try it on a few different sections of your head, since the ends often absorb differently from the roots.

What to do if your tests disagree

This is common. Give more weight to how your hair behaves day to day: how long it takes to dry, whether products sit on top or disappear, and how it handles humidity. If the tests still conflict, you may have a mix, such as a smoother root area and rougher, more processed ends.

Porosity differs across your head and changes over time

Coloring, bleaching, heat styling, sun, weather and even rough brushing can all shift how your hair behaves. Older, more processed lengths often differ from newer growth near the scalp. Retest every few months, or after a big change like a new color, and treat each result as a snapshot.

What should I do with my porosity result?

This is cosmetic routine guidance only. Adjust it to what you see in your own hair, since results vary from person to person.

Low porosity

  • Choose lightweight products such as lotions, mousses and light gels over thick butters and heavy oils.
  • Apply products to damp hair. Some people find gentle warmth, such as a warm towel, helps products spread.
  • Avoid layering many products, which can leave hair feeling coated or weighed down.

Medium porosity

  • This type is usually flexible, so a simple routine of a cleanser, a conditioner and a styler often works.
  • Keep an eye on changes after coloring or frequent heat.

High porosity

  • Try richer creams and butters. Some people also like to follow a water-based product with a light oil or butter to help it last, but how well this works varies, so see whether it suits your hair before making it a habit.
  • Use lower heat settings and a heat protectant, and keep brushing gentle.
  • If your hair dries quickly, you can optionally apply products while it is still very wet, then see whether the finish suits you.

What can't these tests tell me?

They say nothing about hair or scalp health

Porosity tests only describe how your strands take in water. Nothing in this article is medical advice, and FitCheck AI's feedback is cosmetic and not medical advice either, so please see a dermatologist or doctor for any concern about your hair or scalp.

When to get professional advice

Talk to a doctor or dermatologist if you notice a sudden change in texture, unusual shedding, scalp pain, irritation or patches. A porosity test cannot explain those changes.

Frequently asked questions

Is the float test accurate?

It is a rough indicator at best. Product residue, natural oils, strand weight and how long you wait can all change the result, and shed hair may behave differently from hair cut from your head. A floating strand is not proof of low porosity. Test a few strands, combine it with the slip-and-slide or spray bottle test, and pay attention to how your hair behaves in daily life.

How often should I retest my hair porosity?

Every few months is plenty for most people. Retest sooner after coloring, bleaching, a big change in heat styling or a major change in climate, since these can shift how your hair absorbs water. Always test on clean, product-free hair so the results are comparable.

Can coloring, bleaching or heat change my porosity?

They often can. Chemical processing and frequent high heat tend to make the cuticle rougher, so processed hair commonly behaves more like higher porosity hair than untouched hair does. This is why the ends of your hair may test differently from the roots.

Is porosity the same as curl pattern or hair type?

No. Hair type describes strand shape and curl pattern, while porosity describes how readily the strand absorbs and holds moisture. Strand width and density are separate again. Two people with the same curl pattern can have different porosity.
