What Is My Skin Tone? How to Find Your Depth and Undertone

Updated November 5, 2026 · 9 min read

Your skin tone has two parts: depth (how light or deep your skin looks) and undertone (the cool, warm, neutral or olive cast beneath the surface). To work out yours, look at your bare face in natural daylight, name the depth first, then use a few simple checks (veins, jewelry, a white top) to narrow down the undertone. Treat the answer as a starting point for choosing clothes, makeup and hair color, not as a rule.

Skin tone vs. undertone: two different things

Many guides use "skin tone" to mean everything at once, which is why the advice gets confusing. It helps to split it in two.

Depth: how light or deep your skin looks

Depth is the easy part. It describes how light or dark your skin appears overall, usually in loose terms like fair, light, medium, tan or deep. Depth mostly affects how much contrast you can wear. Someone with deep skin and dark hair can often carry very bright or very pale colors, while someone with fair skin and light hair may find very dark shades heavy near the face.

Undertone: the cool, warm, neutral or olive cast beneath the surface

Undertone is the subtle hue under the surface color. Two people with the same depth can have different undertones, which is why the same foundation or lipstick can look right on one and off on the other.

  • Cool: a pink, rosy or bluish cast.
  • Warm: a yellow, peachy or golden cast.
  • Neutral: a balance of the two, with no clear lean either way.
  • Olive: a muted green-gold cast, often described as sitting between warm and neutral.

Why surface color changes but undertone usually stays consistent

Your surface color shifts with sun, season and even a flush after a workout. Undertone is the steadier background hue, which makes it a useful reference for fine-tuning shades once you have considered depth and contrast. It is still a judgment call by eye, which is why the checks below work best when you combine them.

Set up first: how to look at your skin properly

Most wrong answers come from bad viewing conditions, not bad tests. Spend two minutes on setup before you try anything.

Natural daylight, bare face, no filters

Stand by a window in daylight, ideally not in direct harsh sun. Remove makeup, tinted moisturizer and self-tanner if you can. Turn off camera filters and beauty modes, since they smooth and shift color. Warm bulbs add yellow and cool LEDs add blue, so skip indoor lighting for this.

Which areas to check (jawline, neck, inner arm) and why

Check a few spots rather than one. The jawline and neck are useful comparison points next to your cheeks, but necks are often sun-exposed too, so if your neck looks clearly darker or redder than the rest of your face, don't treat it as your baseline. The inner forearm is often more sheltered from sun than the backs of your hands, though this varies from person to person. If the spots look broadly similar in cast, that is a helpful sign.

Why tanning, redness and indoor lighting can mislead you

A recent tan can make you look warmer and deeper than your usual baseline. Temporary flushing from heat, exercise or a hot shower can make skin look pinker, which may push you toward guessing "cool." If either applies, check the more sheltered areas, or repeat your checks in a few days.

How to find your undertone at home

None of these tests is proof on its own. Run all three, note which way each leans, and look for the overall pattern.

The vein check: what it can and can't tell you

Look at the veins on the inside of your wrist in daylight. Veins that look more blue or purple often point cool, and veins that look more green often point warm. If you cannot tell, or see a mix, that can suggest neutral. This check is limited: depth, lighting and how visible your veins are all change what you see, so treat it as one clue.

The jewelry check: silver vs. gold

Hold a silver piece and a gold piece against your bare skin. If silver looks crisper and more flattering, that leans cool. If gold looks richer and warmer on you, that leans warm. If both look fine, you may be neutral. This is also about personal preference, since many people love the metal their undertone "doesn't" favor.

The white paper or white top check

Hold a plain white sheet of paper or a bright white top under your chin, then compare it with an off-white or cream one. Pure white may bring out pink in cooler undertones, while cream can look more harmonious on warmer ones. Pay attention to whether your face looks fresh or slightly washed out, not just to the color of your skin.

What 'neutral' and 'olive' mean, and why they're easy to mix up

Neutral means no strong lean; both warm and cool shades may work for you. Olive has a distinct muted green-gold cast, so it can look "off" in very pink or very orange shades but lovely in earthy tones. People often mistake olive for warm because of the yellow in it, but its greenish note makes some golden shades look sallow while others look rich. How to name your depth

Fair, light, medium, tan, deep: loose descriptions, not strict categories

Compare yourself to the people around you in the same daylight and pick the word that fits best: fair, light, medium, tan or deep. Brands and charts use different names and different numbers of steps, so your label in one place may not match another. That is normal. If you want a more structured vocabulary, the Monk Skin Tone Scale is one widely used 10-shade reference for depth. It is a depth reference, so you still need the undertone checks above.

Why a skin tone chart is a rough reference, not a verdict

A skin tone chart can give you a vocabulary, but screens display color differently, and no chart can account for the full range of human skin. Use it to get close, then rely on how colors look on you in person. Your final answer might be something like "medium depth, warm undertone" or "deep, neutral-cool," and that is plenty of detail to work with.

What to do with your skin tone and undertone

Clothing colors: working with contrast, neutrals and brights

Start with contrast level (your depth, plus how your hair and eyes compare to your skin), then adjust for undertone.

  • Warm undertones often look good in camel, olive green, rust, cream, warm red and mustard. Example: a cream knit, camel trousers and a rust scarf.
  • Cool undertones often look good in navy, charcoal, emerald, cool pink, plum and true white. Example: a navy blazer, white tee and gray trousers, with an emerald top for a bolder look.
  • Neutral undertones usually have the most flexibility, so try both sides and pick by mood.
  • Olive undertones often suit earthy and jewel shades like forest green, burgundy, terracotta and warm taupe. Try them next to your face before committing to very pastel or neon options.

Colors worn near your face matter most, so test a top or scarf at the neckline. For lower-body pieces, wear whatever you like. Makeup: testing foundation on the jawline in daylight

To find a foundation shade, swatch two or three close options along the jawline, not the back of your hand, and check them in daylight. The right one tends to blend into your neck without a visible line. Then consider undertone: a shade that looks slightly gray on you may be too cool, and one that looks yellow or orange may be too warm. For blush and lip, warm undertones often like peach, coral and terracotta, while cool undertones often like rose, berry and cool pink. Neutral and olive skin can usually go either way, so try both. Hair color and jewelry metals: general starting points

Warm undertones often pair well with golden blonde, honey, copper and warm brown, plus gold jewelry. Cool undertones often pair well with ash tones, espresso, blue-black and silver jewelry. Neutral skin can wear mixed metals easily. These are widely used starting points, not requirements. When to break the rules: taste, occasion and personality

Undertone guidance is a shortcut for finding colors that flatter you, but it will not tell you what you enjoy wearing. A color that is "wrong" for your undertone can still work if you keep it away from your face, add a scarf or necklace in a better shade, or simply love it. Occasion matters too: a uniform, a wedding palette or a dress code often outranks any color rule.

Frequently asked questions

Can my skin tone change over time?

Your surface color can shift with the seasons, sun exposure and the way your skin looks day to day, so your depth may look lighter or deeper at different times. Undertone is generally steadier, though perception can change with a tan or temporary flush. If you are unsure, re-check in daylight on a sheltered area like your inner arm.

What if my results are different in different lights?

That is common, because warm bulbs, cool LEDs and filters all push color around. Trust natural daylight over indoor lighting, and repeat your checks at a window at a different time of day. If results still conflict, you may simply be neutral, and a flexible color palette will serve you well.

What if I seem to fit both warm and cool?

This often points to a neutral undertone, which gives you the most room to experiment. Pick colors by what you like and the mood you want, and test them at the neckline. You can also lean on contrast and depth, which often matter as much as undertone.

Do I have to follow my undertone when choosing colors?

No. Undertone is a helpful shortcut, not a requirement. If you love a shade that isn't the textbook match, wear it away from your face, pair it with a flattering color near your neckline, or just enjoy it.
