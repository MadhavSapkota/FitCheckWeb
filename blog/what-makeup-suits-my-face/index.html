What Makeup Suits My Face? A 6-Question Self-Guided Quiz

Updated November 8, 2026 · 9 min read

The makeup that suits your face comes from a combination of undertone, natural contrast, feature placement, and the finish you like, not from face shape alone. Answer the six questions below, jot down your results, and you will end up with a simple makeup profile: a shade family, a level of definition, and a placement plan you can test at home.

How this 'what makeup suits my face' quiz works

What suits you is more than face shape

Many makeup quizzes only ask about face shape, or only about skin tone. Each is a piece of the picture. Undertone and contrast guide color. Face shape and eye shape guide placement. Finish and time guide how the look feels on you day to day. This quiz covers all of them.

How to use the quiz

Write your answer to each question as you go: undertone, contrast level, face shape, eye color and shape, lip preference, finish and time. There is nothing to submit and no score. It is a self-guided checklist in this article.

What you'll end up with

Your result will read something like: warm undertone, medium contrast, oval face, soft-glam everyday look. Then you will turn that into specific products and positions.

Before you start: set up so your answers are accurate

Natural light, clean face, mirror at eye level

Sit facing a window in daylight, with a bare or lightly moisturized face and your mirror at eye level. Overhead bathroom bulbs and yellow lamps can change how skin and hair look.

Skip filters and heavy color-correcting lighting

Beauty filters, ring lights, and strongly tinted lamps can shift your apparent undertone. If you are using photos, take them unfiltered.

Question 1: What is your undertone?

Quick checks

These are informal checks, not proof, so treat each one as a hint.

  • Veins: mostly green may suggest warm, mostly blue or purple may suggest cool, and a mix may suggest neutral.
  • Jewelry: if gold looks richer on you, you may lean warm. If silver does, you may lean cool. If both work, you may be neutral.
  • White vs. cream: stark white may flatter cooler undertones, while ivory and cream may flatter warmer ones.

None of these is definitive, so look for the answer that shows up most often. Our guide to finding your skin tone depth and undertone goes deeper if you are unsure.

What each undertone means for shades

  • Warm: peach or coral blush, golden bronzer, terracotta or brick lips, bronze and copper eyeshadow often work well.
  • Cool: rose or berry blush, soft pink or raspberry lips, taupe, plum, and silver-toned shadows often work well.
  • Neutral: you can borrow from both. Dusty rose, mauve, and true red are reliable starting points.

Question 2: How strong is your natural contrast?

Compare skin, hair, and eyes

Look at how different the depth is between your skin, hair, and eyes. Dark hair with fair skin and light eyes is high contrast. Dark hair, tan skin, and dark eyes, or light hair with light skin and light eyes, is usually lower contrast. Most people land in the middle.

What each level suggests

  • High contrast: you can often carry defined looks well, such as a clear lip, a crisp liner, or deeper shadow.
  • Medium contrast: flexible. Pick one focal point, either eyes or lips, and keep the rest softer.
  • Low contrast: tonal, blended looks tend to feel natural. Think a soft brown liner, a sheer wash of blush, and a lip close to your natural color.

Soft-focus vs. defined

These are tendencies, not rules. If you love a bold lip with low contrast coloring, wear it. Just expect it to read as a statement rather than blend in.

Question 3: What is your face shape, and where does color go?

Face shape as a loose starting point

Pull your hair back and look straight on. Is your face about as wide as it is long (round), longer than it is wide (long), angular at the jaw (square), wider at the forehead than the chin (heart), or gently balanced (oval)? Many people are a blend, so choose the closest.

Blush, bronzer, and highlighter placement ideas

  • Round: sweep blush slightly up and back toward the temples, and keep bronzer light just under the cheekbone.
  • Oval: most placements work. Try blush on the apples blending toward the temples.
  • Square: blend blush on the apples in a soft, rounded shape, and use a little bronzer at the corners of the jaw to soften.
  • Heart: place blush at the apples and a touch of bronzer at the temples and chin to balance.
  • Long: sweep blush more horizontally across the cheeks rather than high on the cheekbone, which adds width.

Highlighter usually goes on the high points of the cheekbones, with a small amount down the nose bridge if you like.

When to ignore face-shape rules

If a placement makes you feel good in the mirror, keep it. Face-shape guidance is a shortcut for balance, not a requirement.

Question 4: What eye color and eye shape are you working with?

Shades that tend to bring out each eye color

  • Blue: warm bronze, copper, and peach.
  • Green: plum, burgundy, and warm brown.
  • Hazel: olive green, gold, and soft purple, depending on which tone you want to emphasize.
  • Brown: very flexible. Try emerald, teal, navy, bronze, or soft berry for contrast.

Simple liner and mascara placement

  • Hooded: apply shadow slightly above the crease so it shows when your eyes are open, and keep liner thin.
  • Wide-set: deepen the inner corners a little.
  • Close-set: brighten the inner corner and concentrate depth on the outer half.
  • Upturned: a smudged, soft line along the lower lashes balances the lift.
  • Downturned: wing liner and mascara slightly upward and outward, and keep the outer lower lash line light.

Glasses wearers

Frames can hide shadow near the brow bone, so slightly more definition on the lid and lashes helps. Blush that sits a touch higher keeps color from disappearing under the frame.

Question 5: What lip color and finish feel like you?

Choose by undertone and contrast

Start with your undertone family from Question 1: coral and brick for warm, berry and cool rose for cool, mauve and true red for neutral. Then use contrast: high-contrast coloring can often wear deeper or brighter shades, and low-contrast coloring often looks best a shade or two from the natural lip.

Sheer, satin, matte, or gloss

This is about comfort and the look you want. Sheer tints are low-effort, satin is a middle ground, matte looks polished and defined, and gloss adds a fuller, fresher look.

Optional tweaks for fuller or thinner lips

Lighter, shinier shades can make lips look fuller. Deeper, matte shades can make them look more defined. Both are purely cosmetic choices.

Question 6: What finish do you prefer, and how much time do you have?

Dewy, natural, or matte

Treat this as a style preference. Dewy looks luminous, natural sits in between, and matte looks smooth and controlled. Pick the one you enjoy wearing.

Routines by time

  • Five minutes: tinted moisturizer or concealer where you want it, cream blush, mascara, tinted lip balm.
  • Fifteen minutes: add foundation, brow fill, a wash of eyeshadow, and a defined lip.
  • Full glam: add contour, highlighter, liner, and lashes, with a setting product where you want it to last.

Occasions

Everyday and work looks usually lean softer and more blended. Date nights and events are where you can raise the definition, either with a deeper lip or a smokier eye, but not both at full volume.

Contrast: how defined to make the look

High contrast: pick a clear focal point. Medium: one focal point, the rest soft. Low: blend everything and stay tonal.

Three sample profiles

  1. Warm, medium contrast, soft everyday glow: a light base, peach cream blush on the apples, a bronze wash on the lids, brown mascara, and a coral satin lip.
  2. Cool, high contrast, defined evening look: a satin base, rose blush, taupe shadow with black liner, and a berry or classic cool red lip.
  3. Neutral, low contrast, five-minute routine: tinted moisturizer, dusty rose cream blush dabbed on the cheeks, soft brown mascara, and a mauve sheer lip.

How to test your results before you commit

Swatch in daylight

Swatch foundation along your jawline and blush or lip color on the inner wrist or the back of your hand, then check them near a window. A foundation shade that seems to disappear into the skin at your jaw is often a good match, though it is worth checking in a second light.

Compare two looks in unfiltered photos

Take an unfiltered photo of each look in the same light and compare them side by side. Photos can show things the mirror does not, such as a blush that is too heavy or a lip that fades.

Change one thing at a time

Swap only the lip, the blush, or the eye, and keep the rest the same. That makes it easy to tell what is working.

Trust comfort

If a look does not feel like you, adjust it. Your quiz result is a starting point, not a rule you have to follow.

Frequently asked questions

Is face shape or skin tone more important for choosing makeup?

They do different jobs. Skin tone and undertone guide which shades look harmonious, while face shape only loosely guides where to place blush, bronzer, and highlighter. If you have to prioritize, start with color and treat face shape as optional fine-tuning.

Can my undertone change with a tan?

Your undertone is generally considered stable, but your surface depth can darken or look warmer after sun exposure. That means you may need a slightly deeper foundation or bronzer in summer while your blush and lip families stay the same.

What if my quiz answers point in different directions?

That is common. A neutral undertone, a mixed face shape, or medium contrast all mean you have flexibility. Pick the answer that appears most often, test two looks side by side in daylight, and go with the one that feels most like you.

How often should I revisit my makeup palette?

Check in when something changes: a new hair color, a big seasonal change in your skin tone, or when a favorite shade stops feeling right. Otherwise, a quick daylight swatch test every few months is plenty.
